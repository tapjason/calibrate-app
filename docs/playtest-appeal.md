# The appeal kit: playtests about whether people want it

Added 2026-11-12 at the owner's request, after the UI-stress round
(`docs/design/research/playtest-2026-11-12.md`). A stress round asks "does it
hold up?"; an appeal round asks **"would this person choose it, enjoy it, come
back to it and tell someone?"**. `/playtest appeal` uses this file: the factors
below pick the cast and the tasks, the block in "For every brief" goes into
each brief word for word, and "Synthesis" says how the answers are counted.
The personas and the cast rules are in `docs/app-profile.md` §10.

**What these numbers are.** The testers are AI agents playing people. Their
appeal answers are a structured way to find **where groups of people part
ways** (tech literacy, age, culture, what they came for) and **the words and
moments that land or sting**. They are not market estimates: never quote a
round's "very disappointed" share or price as what real people would say. The
store's own numbers after launch are the measure (`/live-review`).

## The factors that matter, and how a run catches each

Grouped by when they happen. For each: the question, why it matters for this
app in particular, and what in a run shows it.

### Before the first tap

1. **Arrival sets the expectation.** How someone found the app decides what
   they think it is: App Store search ("a goal tracker?"), a friend's card in
   a group chat ("a personality test?"), a short video about overconfidence
   ("a brain game?"), a family member installing it ("something I'm supposed
   to do"), a therapist or coach suggesting it ("is this a test of me?"), a
   podcast about forecasting ("a serious tool?"). The friend's card is the
   growth loop (GROWTH §0): whether the app delivers what the card promised is
   the share loop's real test. *Caught by:* the brief's "How you found it",
   and "did it deliver what you expected" in the mood line.
2. **The five-second read.** Before any tap: what is it, is it for me, does it
   look made with care, what would I tap. People decide to stay in seconds,
   and a calm screen with no hue can read as premium or as empty. *Caught by:*
   the five-second read in the block.
3. **Looks and taste.** The Instrument direction (D33) is restrained: ink, one
   typeface, colour only for meaning. A designer may admire it; someone who
   loves playful apps (Duolingo, Finch, Co-Star's mystique) may find it cold;
   someone with "plain is fine" taste won't notice. *Caught by:* the three
   words for how it looks, the persona's taste, the word list.

### Time to the first reward

4. **The Day-0 aha.** Does the Warm-up feel like a quiz they'd do for fun
   (Wordle, a pub quiz) or a test they're being marked on? Does the result
   feel like something about *them* ("identity, not statistics") or a
   statistics lesson? How long will a low-literacy or impatient person stay?
   *Caught by:* the mood at the Warm-up's result, "almost left" notes.
5. **The promise, in their words.** Can they say why knowing how sure to be
   helps *them* (a farmer's weather call, a parent's school-run guess, a
   trader's hunch)? If they can't, nothing later matters. *Caught by:* the end
   question "what is it for, for you", the friend sentence.
6. **"Is this for someone like me?"** The starter ideas, the categories (Work,
   Health, Finance, Social, Personal), the examples and the names read as one
   kind of life (an office worker's). A retiree, a farmer, a teenager or a
   shift worker may feel it isn't theirs. *Caught by:* what they choose to
   predict, whether they rename categories, the mood at the first log.

### How it feels to use

7. **Tone when it's bad news.** A miss, "overconfident", a lost streak, a
   score that dropped: kind, fair, cold, preachy, patronising? Directness lands
   differently in Amsterdam, Seoul, Lagos and Ohio. *Caught by:* the mood line
   at a miss, the word list (Kind, Judgemental, Patronising).
8. **Feeling smart or feeling stupid.** Jargon (calibration, lean, Brier, give
   or take), numbers and dense paragraphs make a low-literacy person feel
   tested; dumbing down makes an expert feel talked down to. The same screen
   must respect both. *Caught by:* "words I read twice", mood, the pairs
   confusing–clear and complicated–easy, split by tech literacy.
9. **Delight.** The unlock, a milestone, the Warm-up's real facts, "about 7
   times in 10", a well-made card. Small, earned pleasures are why a utility
   becomes a habit. *Caught by:* "Smile:" notes, the pairs boring–exciting and
   usual–leading edge.
10. **Effort against reward.** Taps per log, the wait until anything is
    answered, twenty answers before a score. Is there something rewarding on a
    day nothing is due? *Caught by:* ease, "what would bring you back
    tomorrow".
11. **Calm, not a chore.** Reminders, streaks and goals can feel like care or
    like nagging; another app asking for daily attention can feel like a debt.
    *Caught by:* mood at reminders and the streak, the words Stressful and
    Calm.

### Identity and other people

12. **Pride in the label.** Would they claim "Forecaster in health"? Do the
    tier names read as cool, childish, nerdy, religious ("Oracle"), or
    meaningless? *Caught by:* mood at the badge, the card answer.
13. **Sharing.** Would they post the card, where (an Instagram story, the
    family WhatsApp, LinkedIn, a team Slack), with what caption, and what
    would stop them: showing a weakness, modesty norms, health or money on
    show, looking like they're advertising. *Caught by:* the card question,
    the culture axis.
14. **Comparison and company.** Do they want to compare with a partner,
    friends or a team, or does doing it alone suit them? The app has no social
    features: is that a relief or a gap? *Caught by:* "Reached for" notes, the
    "one thing that would make me love it".

### Trust and worth

15. **Credibility.** Does the maths feel legitimate (an expert), or like a
    horoscope with numbers (a sceptic)? Does the honesty about luck build
    trust or sound like hedging? *Caught by:* mood at Insights, the words
    Honest and Trustworthy.
16. **Comfort with privacy.** What leaves the phone, health and money titles,
    a shared phone, an account nudge. *Caught by:* notes, the words Safe and
    Intrusive.
17. **Worth paying for.** The free core and a Plus that never blocks: does
    Plus look worth it, at what price, in their currency and against what they
    already pay for (Netflix, Duolingo Super, Headspace)? A price fine in
    London is a week's data in Lagos. *Caught by:* the Plus answer.

### Staying power

18. **Where it fits in a day.** With coffee, on the commute, at a shift break,
    before bed: is there a natural moment, and does the app meet it? *Caught
    by:* "what would bring you back", the reminder they choose.
19. **The pull back.** Tomorrow (an answer due, the practice), next week (the
    recap), next month (a badge, the score settling). *Caught by:* the three
    horizons in the block.
20. **Would they miss it?** The disappointment question (Sean Ellis's product
    test: "how would you feel if you could no longer use it?"). *Caught by:*
    the block.
21. **What they'd say about it.** The one sentence to a friend, and who that
    friend is. *Caught by:* the block.

### What changes the answers (cast these apart)

Tech literacy (five levels, §10), age and life stage, region and culture
(bragging and modesty, directness), money, taste, how they found it, what they
use now, motivation (curious, practical, social, competitive, sceptical,
anxious, playful) and stage (first five minutes, first week, long-time). A
round should be able to say "people at L1–L2 lost interest at X; designers
loved Y; nobody under 25 would post Z".

## For every brief (paste word for word, after block A)

**B. Your first impressions and feelings.** This run is about whether you'd
want this app, not only whether it works.
1. **How you found it** is in "The person" above. Keep it in mind: say along
   the way whether the app is what you expected from that.
2. **The five-second read.** On the first screen, before any tap, write:
   what you think this is; who it's for, and whether that's you; three words
   for how it looks and feels; and whether you'd keep going (yes / maybe /
   no).
3. **Your mood line.** At each of these moments you reach, give your mood
   from −3 (I want out) to +3 (I love this) and a few words why: the first
   screen; the quiz's result (if you see it); your first save; your first
   answer; any time the app tells you something unwelcome (a miss, "too sure",
   a lost streak); the insights; the card; the paid plan.
4. **Smiles and sighs.** Whenever something makes you smile, write
   "Smile: <what>". Whenever you nearly close the app, write "Almost left:
   <what>".
5. **At the end,** after "Would I come back tomorrow?", add this, answered as
   the person you are, in their words:
   ```
   Appeal
   - Five-second read: <what I thought> — keep going: <yes / maybe / no>
   - Mood line: <moment: score, why>; …
   - Eight pairs, 1 to 7 (1 = the left word fits, 7 = the right word fits):
     obstructive–supportive <n>; complicated–easy <n>; inefficient–efficient <n>;
     confusing–clear <n>; boring–exciting <n>; not interesting–interesting <n>;
     conventional–inventive <n>; usual–leading edge <n>
   - Five words that fit it best: <from the list below, or my own>
   - If it disappeared tomorrow I'd be: very disappointed / somewhat disappointed /
     not disappointed — <why>
   - What would bring me back: tomorrow <…>; in a week <…>; in a month <…>
   - What I'd text a friend, and which friend: "<sentence>" (or: I wouldn't, because …)
   - The card: would I post it, where, with what caption — or why not
   - The paid plan: what it's worth to me a year, in my own money, and would I pay
   - The one thing that would make me love it: <…>
   ```
   Words to choose from: Appealing, Boring, Calm, Childish, Clear, Clever,
   Cold, Confusing, Dull, Fresh, Friendly, Fun, Honest, Intimidating,
   Judgemental, Kind, Motivating, Not for me, Patronising, Personal,
   Pointless, Premium, Respectful, Satisfying, Simple, Stressful, Too
   technical, Trustworthy, Useful, Warm.

Adapted from three standard instruments: the eight pairs are the short User
Experience Questionnaire (UEQ-S; the first four measure ease, the last four
appeal), the word list follows Microsoft's product reaction cards, and the
disappointment question is Sean Ellis's product test. Keep the wording above
unchanged between rounds so answers compare.

**Tasks in an appeal brief** are what the person would do with a new app,
not checks: "see if it's any good", "try it with something real from your
week", "show it to your sister", "decide whether it's worth keeping". Four to
six, with at least one about their own life, one about sharing or telling
someone, and one about deciding whether to keep it or pay. The interruption is
real life (a call, a child, the bus stop), and the three end questions still
check comprehension.

**Arriving from a friend's card:** give the text the friend sent, exactly as
the app writes it (`shareText`, `src/components/share/cardCopy.ts`), for
example:

> My calibration · Fairly Sure
> Sharp in health · Tracker in finance
> ⬜⬜🟩🟩🟧 score 87, give or take 4

and who sent it with what message ("my sister: 'what's yours? 😏'"). Start
them on a fresh install.

## Synthesis (for the round's findings file)

Besides the usual usability and comprehension sections:

- **The appeal table:** per run, the five-second keep-going, the lowest and
  highest mood and where, the ease mean (pairs 1–4) and the appeal mean
  (pairs 5–8), the five words, disappointment, would post (where), would pay
  (what).
- **Split it.** Compare by tech literacy (L1–L2, L3, L4–L5), by age (under 26,
  26–60, over 60), by how they found it and by culture. A finding that only
  one group hits is still a finding; say which group.
- **The mood curve.** Where moods dip across runs (the same moment dipping in
  three runs is a P1), and where they peak (protect it).
- **Words.** Count the words chosen; the negative ones with the screens
  behind them.
- **Rank** appeal findings: P1 what loses people before the first reward (the
  five-second read, the Warm-up, the first log); P1 no reason to come back;
  P2 tone and identity (a label nobody would claim, a sting); P2 sharing and
  worth (nobody would post it, Plus worth nothing); P3 polish and delight.
  Mark each "no decision needed" or "needs the owner"; most appeal findings
  need the owner, since they change what a screen says or shows.
- **Never** report the disappointment share or a price as a market number.
